# The State v. the Defendant — Case File

## The Charge

The defendant, a 41-year-old warehouse manager, is charged with arson in the
first degree: deliberately setting fire to Pier 7 Storage & Freight, the
building he managed, in the early hours of a Sunday morning. A guilty verdict
carries a mandatory sentence of twenty-five years. The jury must be unanimous
either way.

## Undisputed Facts

- The fire began in the manager's office at approximately 2:40 a.m. and
  destroyed roughly two-thirds of the warehouse before it was contained.
- The warehouse's fire insurance policy had been increased six weeks earlier,
  raising the payout from $400,000 to $1.1 million. The defendant signed the
  paperwork himself.
- The defendant's key card was used to enter the building at 2:15 a.m. and
  exit at 2:51 a.m., according to the building's access log.
- Fire investigators found a pour pattern consistent with an accelerant along
  the office floor, and lab tests confirmed trace gasoline residue on a
  section of carpet that survived the blaze.
- The business had lost its three largest shipping contracts over the
  previous year and was, by the bookkeeper's account, months from
  insolvency.

## Prosecution Testimony

**The night-shift dockhand** was on a smoke break behind the loading bay and
testified he saw the defendant's car — a distinctive two-tone pickup truck —
parked around the corner from the main entrance at around 2:20 a.m., "same as
always when he came in on a weekend to catch up on paperwork." He did not see
the defendant enter or leave.

**The insurance investigator** testified that the policy increase, timed
against the company's financial position, was "the single largest red flag
I've seen in eleven years in this job," and that in his professional opinion
the fire had "every hallmark of a deliberate loss."

**The fire marshal** testified to the accelerant pour pattern and stated that,
based on burn char depth, the fire "almost certainly" originated at floor
level near the office desk rather than from an electrical fault, though he
conceded under cross-examination that faulty wiring in that section of the
building had been flagged in an inspection eight months earlier and never
fully repaired.

**The locksmith who installed the access system** confirmed that key cards
are personal and non-transferable, but admitted under cross-examination that
a card can be cloned with commercially available equipment in under two
minutes, and that the company had never audited who had access to the card
programming terminal.

## Defense Testimony

**The defendant's alibi** is that he entered the warehouse at 2:15 a.m. to
retrieve a signed contract he'd left in his desk for a Monday morning
meeting, noticed nothing wrong, and left at 2:51 a.m. He says he was home,
asleep next to his wife, by 3:15 a.m., which she confirms.

**On the insurance increase**: the defendant testified the policy was raised
on the broker's recommendation after a stock audit revealed the existing
coverage undervalued the inventory, and that he has the broker's letter
recommending the change, dated two months before he acted on it.

**On the truck**: the defendant says he parked in that spot because the main
lot was being resurfaced that week, which the site foreman corroborates.

**On the wiring**: an electrician who had done prior work in the building
testified that the flagged wiring near the office was original to a 1980s
renovation and had shown signs of insulation breakdown consistent with a
slow electrical fire, though he had not personally inspected it in the weeks
before the blaze.

## Points Raised at Trial

- No accelerant container, gas can, or purchase record was ever recovered or
  traced to the defendant.
- The defense's electrician was paid as an expert witness by the defendant's
  attorney, which the prosecution highlighted in cross-examination.
- The insurance investigator conceded he had recommended denying claims in
  fires he later admitted, under further questioning, were accidental.
- Company financial records show the defendant personally stood to gain
  nothing directly from the insurance payout — it would have gone to the
  business, in which he holds no ownership stake, only a salaried position.
